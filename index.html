🗺️ A suggested route shape

For travellers wanting the broader picture rather than just Seoul and Jeju, here's a first-timer's loop that covers all five regions in this guide without doubling back. Treat it as a skeleton, not a booking.

✈️ Arrive Seoul → 🏙️ Seoul (3-4 nights) → 🪖 DMZ (day trip) → 🚄 KTX to Gyeongju → 🏛️ Gyeongju (1-2 nights) → 🚄 KTX to Busan → 🌊 Busan (2 nights) → 🛫 Fly to Jeju → 🌋 Jeju Island (3-4 nights) → ✈️ Fly home via Seoul/Busan

Gyeongju and Busan sit on the same KTX line, so that leg is simple. Jeju is fly-only from the mainland, no bridge or ferry for passengers doing it quickly, so budget a short domestic flight each way (about an hour from Seoul or Busan).

📶 Before You Go: the five-minute version

  • UK passport holders: visa-free for 90 days, K-ETA currently waived through end of 2026, but everyone still completes the online e-Arrival Card before landing.
  • Get an eSIM sorted before you fly; land already connected.
  • Download KakaoMap or Naver Map before you arrive, Google Maps is unreliable for walking/transit directions inside Korea.
  • Cash still matters for small vendors and traditional markets, but T-money (transit) and card payments cover almost everything else.
  • Tipping is not expected anywhere, including restaurants and taxis.

PracticalWhen to go

Spring (March-May): cherry blossoms peak late March to early April, earlier in Jeju and Busan, later in Seoul. Beautiful, but peak tourist season, book flights and hotels early.

Summer (June-August): the monsoon (jangma) usually runs late June to late July with heavy rain, followed by high heat and humidity into August. Typhoon season peaks in August into early September, mainly affecting Jeju and the southern coast, worth keeping an eye on if you're travelling then. Generally considered the toughest season for sightseeing.

Autumn (September-November): widely rated the best season, cool, dry, clear skies. Foliage peaks mid-October to early November on the mainland, a little later in Jeju.

Winter (December-February): cold, dry, and Jeju/Gangwon-do (Vivaldi Park) get proper ski season. Fewer crowds at major sights.

PracticalHolidays to plan around

Two dates matter more than any other for timing a trip, both multi-day family holidays when a huge share of the country travels home at once: expect packed trains, jammed highways, and many shops/restaurants closed, especially outside big cities.

Seollal (Lunar New Year): 16-18 February 2026.

Chuseok (harvest festival): 24-26 September 2026.

Neither date is fixed, both follow the lunar calendar and shift every year, so if your trip is a way off, check the current dates rather than trusting these ones blindly.

PracticalEntry & visa

As a UK citizen you can enter South Korea visa-free for stays up to 90 days. K-ETA (the electronic travel authorisation) is normally required for visa-free entry, but that requirement has been temporarily waived for UK nationals through 31 December 2026. From 1 January 2027 K-ETA becomes mandatory again, so check the date against your travel plans closer to the time.

Separately, and regardless of K-ETA: everyone completes the Q-code / e-Arrival Card online before landing. This replaced the old paper arrival card and is a straightforward digital form, not the same thing as K-ETA.

Bring a printed or digital return/onward ticket confirmation, it's occasionally asked for at immigration.

PracticalGetting connected: eSIM vs airport SIM

For a trip like this, an eSIM bought and installed before you fly is the easier option. You land already online instead of queuing at an airport kiosk after a long flight.

One real limitation worth knowing: prepaid tourist SIMs (eSIM or physical) are tied to your passport data and legally capped at 90 days, and they can't complete Korea's identity verification step ("bonin-injeong") that local apps like Coupang or Yogiyo need for full account use, that specifically requires a long-term residency card (ARC). Doesn't affect a normal trip, but explains why some apps stay half-locked no matter how good your data connection is.

Two ecosystems, not eight separate apps. KakaoTalk, KakaoMap and Kakao T all run on one Kakao account, and Kakao Pay (in-store QR payments) sits on that same login once you have it. Naver Map and Papago run on one Naver account the same way, with Naver Pay on top, and 2026 updates made both noticeably more tourist-friendly: Kakao and Naver signups no longer strictly need a Korean phone number, and Naver added passport verification plus wider overseas card acceptance for Naver Pay specifically. Worth setting up one Kakao account and one Naver account properly before you fly, rather than using each app as an anonymous guest, that's what actually unlocks the payment and booking features, not just the maps and translation. T-money works on a similar principle of its own: one card or Apple Wallet entry, not tied to Kakao or Naver, covers subway, bus, some taxis and convenience stores nationwide.

PracticalMoney & cards

  • Card payment is near-universal in cities, contactless works widely.
  • Carry some cash for traditional markets, small street food stalls and some rural areas on Jeju.
  • A T-money card (physical, or via Apple Wallet/the Tmoney app) covers subway, bus and even some taxis and convenience stores. Top up at any convenience store or subway machine.
  • NOL World card is worth getting at Incheon if you'd rather not deal with a separate currency exchange stop.
  • Tipping is not customary anywhere in Korea, restaurants, taxis, hotels, and can come across as odd rather than generous.

PracticalGeneral packing checklist

  • Passport with at least 6 months' validity left
  • Phone with eSIM installed and activated before you fly
  • Plug adapter: Korea uses Type C/F sockets (220V, 60Hz), UK plugs (Type G) need an adapter, but UK electronics don't need a voltage converter since 220-240V is compatible
  • Portable power bank, useful for long sightseeing days between charging stops
  • Comfortable walking shoes, expect a lot of subway stairs and cobbled palace/market ground
  • Layers, weather varies more than you'd expect between Seoul, the coast and Jeju, even in the same week
  • Travel insurance details and a photo or copy of your passport's photo page, kept separately from the passport itself
  • T-money card, or set it up in Apple Wallet before you land if you're on iPhone
  • Small day bag for day trips (DMZ, Nami Island, Gyeongju)

PracticalSmall extras worth packing

  • A small packet of tissues, useful given how rare street bins are (see Daily-life practicalities below), and some café/public toilets run out of paper towels.
  • A reusable shopping bag, convenience stores and supermarkets don't hand out free carrier bags.
  • Don't count on public USB charging being available, it isn't common, bring your own power bank (already on the checklist above).
  • In winter, many cafés provide blankets at outdoor or window seating, a nice surprise rather than something to pack, but don't assume it and skip a scarf.
  • In summer, portable handheld fans are sold everywhere (convenience stores, department stores, street stalls), cheap enough to just buy on arrival rather than pack.

PracticalExtra to bring for specific stops

  • DMZ tour: physical passport is mandatory, no digital copy accepted. Dress within the code (no sleeveless tops, shorts, sandals, or military-style clothing), checked at the door.
  • Palaces & temples (Bukchon, Gyeongbokgung, Bulguksa): modest clothing (shoulders/knees covered) and shoes you don't mind removing at floor-seating areas.
  • Hallasan: proper hiking shoes, water and layers, plus a plan, this one now needs a reservation booked in advance (opens monthly), and there's a strict checkpoint cutoff time to reach the mid-mountain shelter, missing it by even a minute turns you back regardless of how far you've come. Cutoff times shift by season, check the official time the night before you go.
  • Jeju rental car: passport, UK driving licence, and a physical International Driving Permit (1968 Vienna Convention) arranged before you fly, see the Jeju tab.
  • Beaches (Haeundae, Jeju coast): swimwear, reef-safe sunscreen, some cash for beach vendors.
  • Nami Island / other outdoor day trips: comfortable shoes and a layer for the boat crossing, it's breezier on the water.

PracticalWeather prep: monsoon & deep winter

🌧️ Summer monsoon (Jangma), late June to late July: not light showers, torrential and relentless, with humidity regularly above 85%. Flash flooding can shut down rural mountain trails and bus routes with little warning. Skip the heavy waterproof jacket, it's too hot, a cheap sturdy umbrella from any CU/GS25/7-Eleven (around ₩5,000) does the job. Quick-drying mesh shoes or waterproof sandals beat anything leather, humidity can grow mould on leather within days.

📝 Traveller's Note

Don't write off a rainy day here. I love rain, and some of Korea's best moments happen in it: palaces and temples empty out and feel completely different once the crowds clear for the weather.

❄️ Deep winter (Dong-guk), December to February: Seoul and the northern regions regularly drop to -10°C to -15°C, sharpened by dry winds off Siberia. A long, ankle-length padded down jacket, the "Long Padding" (롱패딩), is the unofficial national winter uniform for a reason, worth having thermal base layers, a scarf and windproof gloves alongside it. On the coast (Yangyang and similar, see Hidden Coastal Gems), pavements don't get cleared of ice quickly, shoes with real rubber grip matter more than they would in Seoul.

Ondol floor heating: hanoks and many Korean homes heat from underfloor (see Where to Stay below), heat rises directly through the floorboards. Don't leave luggage, plastics or chocolate sitting directly on the floor overnight, they can warp or melt. Wall-mounted heating/cooling panels are usually Korean-only, the three symbols worth recognising are 운전 (On), 정지 (Off) and 실내 (Room temperature).

PracticalSeasonal annoyances: mosquitoes & spring dust

Mosquitoes: a real summer presence, especially near the Han River in Seoul and around Jeju's countryside, pack repellent or pick some up at any convenience store.

Spring dust: "hwangsa" (yellow dust) and fine particulate pollution both spike in spring, drifting in from the continent. Check the air quality forecast on the days you're out and about, and bring a KF94 mask if you're sensitive, they're sold everywhere from convenience stores to pharmacies.

PracticalGetting from the airport

Incheon (ICN) is where almost all international flights land. Gimpo (GMP) handles mostly domestic and a few regional international routes.

AREX Express: runs Incheon T1/T2 to Seoul Station in about 43 minutes, non-stop, doesn't call at Gimpo.

AREX All-Stop: slower, but does serve Gimpo, about 22 minutes from there to Seoul Station.

Buying an Express ticket also gets you access to the City Airport Terminal at Seoul Station, where you can check luggage, get your boarding pass and clear immigration hours before your flight, worth it on the way home.

Airport limousine buses are the other option, slower but drop closer to hotels if you'd rather not handle luggage on the train.

PracticalAccessibility notes

Lifts exist in most subway stations but aren't always obviously signposted, look for the wheelchair symbol near the ticket gates rather than assuming step-free access is right by the entrance. Larger interchange stations are accessible but can mean genuinely long walks between platforms. Older palace and hanok village areas have uneven stone paths and raised thresholds, manageable with a bit of care but worth knowing about in advance if mobility is a concern.

PracticalWhere to stay: accommodation types

A hanok stay puts you in a traditional house, often with ondol underfloor heating and a thin mat on the floor instead of a bed, a genuine cultural experience rather than just a place to sleep.

A guesthouse (common in Hongdae/Sinchon) gives you Airbnb-style social atmosphere with proper hotel-style legal registration behind it.

A Korean motel, sometimes still called a "love motel", is a completely different thing to the UK connotation: clean, modern, walk-in without a reservation, often with a bathtub and fast wifi, and genuinely used by ordinary travellers and families, not just couples, the stigma is outdated.

Airbnb specifically is getting more restricted: Korea requires hosts to hold proper business registration, and by 2026 a large share of previously-listed officetel/studio-flat Airbnbs have been delisted for being unlicensed.

If you book an Airbnb, check it's a registered "Foreigner Tourist Urban Homestay" or similar rather than an ordinary flat, or just book a hotel/guesthouse/hanok directly to sidestep the question.

PracticalLaundry

Most hotels charge steeply for in-house laundry. Coin launderettes ("dongjeon sedaksho", 동전 세탁소) are common in residential neighbourhoods and genuinely useful for a longer stay, self-service, card or coin payment, a wash-and-dry cycle runs roughly ₩6,000-10,000 all in. Some guesthouses and hanoks include a washing machine for guest use, worth asking about when you book if you're staying more than a few nights.

PracticalCash, ATMs & getting a tax refund

Not every ATM accepts foreign cards, look for ones marked "Global ATM" (common in banks, post offices and 24-hour convenience stores like CU/GS25/7-Eleven, which tend to be more reliable for overseas cards than ordinary bank machines).

Per-transaction withdrawal limits sit around ₩1,000,000, and Korean ATMs don't accept PINs longer than 4 digits, worth knowing before you queue. Most bank-branch ATMs go offline briefly overnight (roughly 11:50pm-12:30am) for daily settlement.

Also worth doing: the tourist VAT refund. If your total stay is under 6 months, you can reclaim part of the 10% VAT on qualifying purchases (minimum spend around ₩15,000-30,000 per store, varies by shop).

Refund operators Global Blue, Global Tax Free and Easy Tax Refund issue the paperwork in-store, actual cash back after their fee is usually 4-7%, not the full 10%. Process the refund at the Tax Refund Kiosk before you check in your luggage at the airport, it needs your passport and receipts.

PracticalDaily-life practicalities

  • Free public wifi: the Seoul Wi-Fi app covers over 10,000 free hotspots, including every subway station, plus parks and tourist attractions citywide, look for networks named "Seoul_Free_WiFi" or "Public WiFi@Seoul". Handy as an eSIM backup.
  • Rubbish separation is genuinely strict: general waste needs a specific coloured bag sold locally (and it's district-specific, a bag bought in one area technically isn't valid in another), recycling gets sorted by material and needs to be reasonably clean (a greasy pizza box is general waste, not recycling), and food waste goes in its own separate bag or smart bin. If you're in a guesthouse or Airbnb with shared bins, ask your host which is which rather than guessing.
  • Street bins are rare, not missing by accident: public rubbish bins on Seoul's streets are genuinely hard to find, it's deliberate policy, not an oversight. Subway station platforms and convenience store entrances are the two reliable spots to offload street rubbish while you're out, hang onto anything else until you reach one.
  • Time zone: Korea Standard Time is UTC+9 year-round, no daylight saving. That's 9 hours ahead of the UK in winter (GMT) and 8 hours ahead in British Summer Time.
  • Ordering kiosks: unmanned self-order touchscreens are now the default at cafes, fast-casual chains and a growing number of full-service restaurants, over 80,000 venues nationwide by early 2026. Most have an English-language toggle in a corner of the screen, order, pay by card at the kiosk, and take your buzzer or receipt to the counter. Some coffee shops and "study cafe" reading rooms are entirely unstaffed, badge in and pay the same way.
  • Currency quick maths: as of late July 2026, £1 is roughly ₩1,950-2,050, so a fast mental trick is to drop the last three zeros and roughly halve what's left (₩20,000 ≈ £10, ₩6,000 ≈ £3). The rate moves, so check a live conversion before you travel rather than trusting this exactly.
  • Toilets: all public toilets are free, and since a 2018 rule most modern buildings (hotels, malls, subway stations, newer restaurants) take toilet paper down the pipe same as the UK. If you see a small bin next to the toilet, mostly in older buildings, use it, older plumbing genuinely can't handle paper. Squat toilets are increasingly rare but still turn up in older buildings or rural areas, face the hooded/drain side if you meet one.
  • Tap water: generally safe to drink straight from the tap nationwide, though many locals still prefer filtered or bottled water out of habit rather than a safety concern. Fine either way.

Hidden GemKorean convenience stores are their own attraction

Worth treating as a genuine experience, not just a backup option, they're a different category of shop entirely to a UK Tesco Express. CU, GS25, 7-Eleven and E-Mart 24 (open 24/7, 365 days a year) function as tiny kitchens: microwaves and hot-water dispensers at a dedicated noodle station let you cook instant ramyeon on the spot, CU's "Ramyeon Library" concept stores stock over 225 varieties.

Dosirak (lunchbox meals, rice with multiple sides, pickles, a soft-boiled egg, fishcake) are a proper meal for ₩4,000-6,000, not a sad sandwich equivalent. Most branches have a few seats to eat in, some stock alcohol and let you drink it there. New products drop every Tuesday and Thursday, so the shelves genuinely change week to week.

Korea has the highest convenience store density in the world, about one for every 950 people, prices are fixed nationwide, no tourist markup anywhere. Worth popping into more than one chain even if you're not buying anything, the differences between CU and GS25's food ranges are a small rabbit hole of their own.

Luggage shipping (Taekbae): heading somewhere like Yangyang (see Hidden Coastal Gems) and don't want to haul a suitcase on rural buses? Many CU and GS25 branches have a "Postbox" kiosk: select English, weigh your bag on the attached scale, type in the destination address, pay a small fee (usually under ₩10,000) at the till. It typically arrives within 24-48 hours.

The terrace rule: rural branches especially tend to have plastic tables and umbrellas set up outside, and it's completely normal to sit and eat or drink there, a genuinely cheap alternative to a bar.

1+1 and 2+1 promotions: check the shelf tags, "1+1" means buy one, get a second free, "2+1" means buy two, get a third. Mixing flavours within the same brand is usually fine.

At the ramyeon station specifically, the rubbish bins are split three ways: liquid/food waste, recyclable plastic, and paper packaging, clean up after yourself the same way the general rubbish-separation rule (Daily-life practicalities, above) describes.

Hidden GemBudget travel: free & cheap culture

From April 2026, "Culture Day" (discounted or free entry to museums, cinemas and performances nationwide) runs every Wednesday rather than just the last Wednesday of the month. Free palace admission is being phased in on top of that: Deoksugung from August 2026, Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung from October 2026, with Changgyeonggung and Deoksugung open until 9pm on their free Wednesdays. Gyeongbokgung's free-Wednesday date hasn't been set yet, still under review over crowding concerns, so don't assume it's included until it's confirmed nearer the time.

The National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art adds free entry until 9pm on Wednesdays and Saturdays too.

May adds a nationwide "Museum and Art Gallery Week" with further free access.

Combine with the hanbok-for-free-palace-entry trick (Seoul tab) and a genuinely cultural day can cost almost nothing.

PracticalBudget travel: transport passes & cheap eats

Seoul's Climate Card now does short-term tourist passes: 1-day (₩5,000), 3-day (₩10,000) or 5-day (₩15,000) for unlimited subway, bus and public bike use, worth it if you're moving around a lot in a short window.

The Discover Seoul Pass bundles free entry to 70+ attractions (palaces, N Seoul Tower, and more) over 24/48/72 hours and can be paired with the Climate Card.

For food, a realistic daily food budget is around ₩20,000-30,000 for three solid meals, helped by no tipping and free banchan (side dish) refills everywhere. Ramyeon runs about ₩1,500, a bowl of bibimbap around ₩6,000. Convenience store meals (dosirak lunchboxes, kimbap, tteokbokki) are genuinely decent, not just filler, at ₩2,000-5,000.

The single best-value tip: department store basement food courts (Lotte, Shinsegae, and others) serve proper cafeteria-style Korean, Japanese and Chinese food at a fraction of what the restaurant floors above charge, worth seeking out over the food court in a regular shopping mall.

🙇 Culture & Etiquette 24 min read

Koreans are generally forgiving of tourist mistakes, but a little effort goes a long way.

Hanbok, Korea's traditional dress. Photo via Unsplash.

CultureGreetings

A small nod (15-20°) is fine for casual moments, thanking a shopkeeper, greeting staff. A deeper bow (30-45°) is for older people, hosts, or when someone's done you a real favour. As a tourist you're not expected to bow formally, a nod and a smile covers most interactions.

CultureShoes

Take shoes off entering someone's home, an Airbnb, or traditional floor-seating restaurants. Look for a raised entryway or a shelf of shoes by the door, it's a reliable cue.

CultureDining

Never leave chopsticks standing upright in a rice bowl, that's reserved for funeral rites. Meals are often shared and one person covers the whole bill, with the group taking turns treating each other across a trip rather than splitting every time. Don't feel obliged to match this as a visitor, just know it's the norm you're seeing.

The shared pot: stews (jjigae) often arrive in one bubbling pot for the whole table, and everyone dips their own spoon straight in. If that's not for you, a small side bowl (Ap-je, 앞접시) is usually nearby, ask for one and use the serving spoon to portion out your share first, no one will think it's odd.

Wait for the eldest: don't pick up your chopsticks or spoon until the oldest person at the table has taken their first bite, and don't get up to leave until they've finished eating either.

The scissors: don't be surprised if your server cuts your meat, cold noodles or kimchi at the table with a pair of metal scissors, it's normal and practical, let them get on with it rather than trying to tear the food apart with chopsticks.

Ssam and ssamjang: at a barbecue table (samgyeopsal especially, see Iconic dishes), wrapping is as much the meal as the meat. A ssam is a lettuce or perilla leaf loaded with grilled meat, rice, garlic and a dab of ssamjang, a fermented soybean-and-chilli paste, folded up and eaten in one bite, no cutlery finesse required. It's near-universal wherever you're eating Korean BBQ, not a regional or restaurant-specific thing, so expect the leaf basket and the little dish of paste as standard, not an extra.

CultureMoney manners

No tipping, anywhere. Handing over or receiving cash/cards with two hands (or one hand supporting the other arm) is a small respectful gesture, especially with someone older.

CultureWho pays? Being invited out for a meal

If you're lucky enough to be invited out, by Korean colleagues, or new friends made while travelling, the bill comes with its own etiquette. Splitting evenly per item isn't the norm here.

The inviter pays: whoever suggested the meetup or extended the invitation is expected to cover the whole bill, not just their share.

Age and seniority: dining with someone older, or a more senior work contact, that person will usually pay regardless of who suggested it.

Taking turns: among close friends or peers of similar standing, one person covers the whole bill each outing, and it rotates across future meals rather than splitting every single time.

The bill dance: expect a polite tug-of-war over the cheque. As the guest, offer to pay or chip in, that's the right move, but let the host insist if they're older or invited you, that's the expected outcome, not a bluff.

Reciprocating: rather than pushing cash on them there and then, the polite move is to offer to treat them to the next meal or coffee instead.

With new friends specifically: whoever invited you will almost certainly insist on paying for the first round. If the group moves on to a second location for coffee, dessert or drinks, a "2-cha" (2차), that's your moment to step in and pay for that round. If the night ends after the first meal, a warm thank-you and a promise to cover the next hangout does the job.

N-Bbang (N빵): the modern exception, splitting the bill evenly by headcount. Increasingly common among younger Koreans and peer groups specifically to avoid loading the cost onto one person. One person pays the full bill at the till, then everyone transfers their share afterwards via a banking app, as a visitor without a Korean banking app, offering cash for your share works just as well.

CultureTemples & palaces

Dress modestly, shoulders and knees covered is the safe default. Keep voices down inside temple grounds. Photography is usually fine outdoors but check for signs restricting it indoors or around active worship areas.

CultureDarye: the Korean tea ceremony

Korea has its own tea ceremony, darye (다례, "tea etiquette"), rooted in Buddhist and Confucian ritual. It's simpler and less formal than Japan's better-known version, less about a fixed sequence of movements and more about a quiet, shared moment between host and guest.

The Korean Tea Culture Center in Seoul runs demonstrations and workshops, and traditional teahouses around Insadong and Bukchon Hanok Village serve tea the traditional way if you'd rather experience it than study it.

For something quieter, some temples run a tea session with a monk through the Templestay programme (templestay.com), a more contemplative version of the same tradition. On Jeju, the O'Sulloc Tea Museum runs its own guided ceremony among the green tea fields, see the Jeju tab.

For a more contemporary take, OMOT near Seoul Forest in Seongsu-dong runs a private, storytelling-style tea course, around 90 minutes, pairing seasonal teas with traditional dasik sweets. Reservation-only, via Naver Talk or Instagram DM, a modern counterpoint to Insadong's traditional teahouses.

CulturePublic transport

Priority seating (often pink or a different colour) is genuinely respected, don't sit there even if empty. Phone calls on the subway are generally avoided or kept very quiet.

Queueing: platform floor markings show where doors will open, queue there rather than clustering in front of the whole car, and let people off before you board. Quietly, no pushing, even when it's packed.

Escalators: stand on one side, walk on the other, the convention is usually stand-right in Seoul, though it varies slightly by city and isn't strictly enforced everywhere, watch what everyone else is doing and match it.

CultureDrinking

Never pour your own drink, someone else fills your glass and you fill theirs, keep an eye on others' glasses and top them up before they empty. When an elder or senior pours for you, hold your glass with two hands (or your right hand supported by your left).

If you're drinking in front of someone notably older or more senior, it's polite to turn your body slightly away as you drink. Anju (안주), snacks or a proper dish, goes with the drinks, drinking without food alongside is unusual and can look reckless. As a visitor, no one expects you to get every rule right, the effort is what lands.

CultureThe soju tornado

Before pouring, you'll often see someone spin the soju bottle, either by rolling it fast between their palms or twisting a spoon or chopstick inside the neck, to whip the liquid into a visible vortex.

It looks like showmanship, and now it partly is, but it started as something practical: in the years after the Korean War, soju bottles were cork-sealed and could pick up bits of sediment, so people swirled the bottle to spin any debris to the surface, then flicked or tapped it out before pouring a clean shot.

Modern bottles use metal caps and filtered spirit, so that original problem hasn't existed for decades, and the brand Chum-Churum leaned into the ritual in its marketing, claiming the spin improved the taste, which helped it stick around long after the reason for it disappeared.

These days it's pure ritual: a shared little performance that marks the start of a meal and sets the mood, not something that changes what's in the glass. If a table gets a soju tornado going, you'll usually see everyone genuinely enjoying the small ceremony of it, worth a smile even if the drink itself isn't your thing.

FoodIconic dishes

  • Kimchi: fermented, seasoned cabbage, turns up at almost every meal as a side dish, it's the one most people have heard of, but it's worth trying a few regional or seasonal versions rather than assuming it's all the same.
  • Kimbap: seaweed rice rolls, a full packed-lunch meal in a roll.
  • Mandu: dumplings, fried or steamed, look out for kimchi-filled ones.
  • Bibimbap: mixed rice with vegetables, meat and gochujang.
  • Samgyeopsal: grilled pork belly, cook-it-yourself at the table, wrapped up ssam-style with lettuce and ssamjang (see Dining, above).
  • Kimchi-bokkeumbap: kimchi fried rice, comfort food.
  • Korean fried chicken: double-fried, thinner and crunchier than the UK version, often ordered alongside beer as a pairing called chimaek. It's also the reigning star of mukbang and "ASMR chicken" videos, that thin, crackly crust is exactly what makes the crunch so satisfying on camera, not a different dish, just the same chicken with a microphone pointed at it. Mukbang itself is a Korean-coined term (from meokda, "to eat", and bangsong, "broadcast") for the eating-show genre, it made it into the Oxford English Dictionary in 2021, several years ahead of noraebang (see Entertainment & Nightlife).
  • Korean-style pizza: a genuinely different animal from Italian or UK pizza, built around a sweet-savoury contrast, think corn, sweet potato, bulgogi, potato wedges and even honey alongside the cheese. Mr. Pizza is the pioneer and best-known chain, famous for a sweet-potato-mousse-stuffed crust, worth ordering once purely for the novelty (see Entertainment & Nightlife and Before You Go for how easily it gets delivered almost anywhere).

FoodStreet food: the trinity and beyond

Every pojangmacha (street cart) and bunsik stall in the country sells the same core trio side by side, order any one and you'll be offered the other two.

  • Tteokbokki: chewy rice cakes in spicy gochujang sauce (see the Seoul tab).
  • Eomuk-guk / odeng-guk: skewered fish cake simmered in a warm, savoury broth, the broth itself is often self-serve and free alongside a skewer, the everyday street-cart soup.
  • Twigim: assorted deep-fried snacks, vegetables, mandu, squid, sweet potato, the third leg of the trinity, usually dunked in the tteokbokki sauce.
  • Sundae: Korean blood sausage, glass noodles and pig's blood packed into intestine casing, steamed and sliced, served with liver and lung and a dip of salt or ssamjang. Polarising, but worth trying once, it's not hidden away, it's a proper street-stall regular.
  • Korean corn dog: the internationally famous one, rice-battered, some rolled in sugar, some with a cheese-pull core, a genuine modern street food phenomenon rather than just a tourist photo-op.
  • Hotteok: a fried pancake filled with melted brown sugar and cinnamon syrup, a winter street classic (Busan does a savoury seed-filled version, ssiat hotteok, worth comparing).
  • Bungeoppang: fish-shaped pastry filled with sweet red bean, another cold-weather street snack. Its cousin gyeran-ppang swaps the red bean for a whole baked egg, same stalls, same season, different filling.
  • Kkwabaegi: a twisted, sugar-glazed dough doughnut, cheap, chewy and sold everywhere, easy to miss between the bigger names on this list but worth grabbing if you spot one.

PracticalTravelling solo? You're covered

Solo travel here is genuinely easy, and it doesn't get talked about enough in guides like this one.

  • Eating alone is completely normal in cafés, no one bats an eye at a table for one.
  • Some Korean BBQ restaurants still require a minimum of two portions, but many now welcome solo diners, worth checking the menu or asking before you sit down.
  • Convenience stores and department store food halls (see Before You Go and above) are excellent solo meal options, no ordering-for-one awkwardness at all.
  • Study cafés and book cafés (below) are comfortable places to spend an hour alone, reading, working or just resting between sights.

CultureCoffee culture: Korea's real national pastime

Korea takes cafés further than almost anywhere else. Beyond the usual flat white, expect a genuine subculture built around distinct café types, each with its own following.

The single most useful thing to know: staying for hours over one drink is completely normal here. Working remotely on a laptop, studying, or just settling in for an afternoon are all expected uses of a café table, not exceptions, and most places genuinely don't rush you to free it up. Worth knowing if you need somewhere comfortable to wait out weather, charge devices, or just take a breather between sights.

Cat cafés let you order a coffee and sit with resident cats free to roam. Hongdae has several well cared-for ones, some doubling as reading spots with comics and novels on the shelves.

Book cafés lean into towering wall-to-wall shelving and hushed reading-room seating, a proper place to disappear into a book for an afternoon rather than just a coffee stop.

Study cafés (독서실 dokseosil, or the newer 카공 cagong culture) are a distinctly Korean institution: quiet, laptop-friendly spaces, sometimes with partitioned desks or timed private rooms, rented by the hour. They exist because studying, for school, university, or professional licences, continues well into adult life here, and a quiet desk is worth paying for in a city where many homes are compact. Cheap public study spaces have sprung up too, popular enough to have sparked real pushback from nearby café owners losing custom to them, a genuine 2026 local news story, not just a quirky aside.

Rooftop and river-view cafés trade on the view as much as the coffee, expect to pay a premium for a window seat.

CultureBook culture: megastores, book cafés & stationery

Beyond the book cafés above, Korea takes bookshops and stationery seriously enough that browsing one is a proper afternoon out in its own right, not just an errand. Kyobo Book Centre's flagship, underneath Gwanghwamun Square, is the one to build a stop around: around 2.3 million volumes across a 9,000m² basement floor, one of the country's largest foreign-language sections, and a genuinely large stationery hall (문구장, "Mungujang") of curated pens, calligraphy sets and desk accessories rather than the usual rack of biros. Reachable directly from exits 4 and 5 of Gwanghwamun Station.

Stationery has its own cult following here, distinct from the bookshops. Artbox and Morning Glory are the cute-and-affordable chains aimed at students and browsers, expect character goods, washi tape and diary-decorating supplies alongside the basics. Alpha's Namdaemun flagship is the serious end of the same hobby, four floors split by type (office supplies, art and craft, paper, files), aimed at people who take their desk setup seriously rather than tourists, worth it if that's you.

Hidden GemStarfield Library, Coex

A genuinely free public library inside the Coex Mall in Gangnam, two-storey shelving stretching floor to ceiling, reading tables scattered through the open atrium, and one of the most photographed interior spaces in Seoul, purely as a piece of architecture rather than a bookshop to buy from. Worth combining with a Coex wander generally (see K-Pop, for its merch pop-ups) since it sits inside the same complex.

PracticalReceipt secrets: Wi-Fi codes & toilet doors

Don't bin your receipt the second you've paid. At most Korean cafés, Starbucks and A Twosome Place included, the Wi-Fi password and, more usefully, the toilet door code are printed right at the bottom of it. Toilets are often locked with a keypad rather than left open, and first-time visitors regularly find this out only once they're already stood outside a locked door with the receipt in the bin.

Order numbers: kiosks print a number on your receipt, but pickup works two ways depending on the venue, some flash it on an overhead LED screen, others call it out over a speaker in Korean. If it's the speaker version and you miss it, just watch the counter and match your number as trays come up, no one minds you double-checking.

CulturePublic displays of affection

Handholding is fine and common, but kissing, prolonged hugging or generally passionate PDA in public will draw genuine stares, not just mild disapproval.

It comes from the same Confucian-influenced emphasis on modesty and proper public conduct that shapes a lot of Korean social etiquette, older generations in particular can find it disrespectful rather than just a bit much, and Korean culture more broadly treats physical space and touch (even non-romantic) as something to keep contained in public.

It's loosening among younger couples in nightlife areas like Hongdae, but the safe default as a visitor is to save anything beyond handholding for a private spot, a park bench or a café rather than the street. Couples here often show affection in other visible ways instead, matching outfits is a genuine, common thing.

CultureCouple culture: rings, rituals & where to go

Korean couples count relationship days from scratch, day one (일일, il-il) is the day you became official, not the first date. The 100-day anniversary is the first big milestone, treated as proof the relationship has staying power, historically echoing the traditional 100-day marker for a newborn surviving its most vulnerable stretch. Couples mark it with a proper date and, very commonly, matching couple rings, 200, 300, 500 and 1,000-day anniversaries follow on the same pattern.

You can make your own on the spot: Ring University (Myeongdong) and Banji Cafe (Hongdae) both run walk-in, pure-silver ring-making sessions with laser engraving, birthstones and diamond-setting available as add-ons (roughly ₩30,000 per extra), no jewellery-making experience required, staff talk you through it.

Combine with what's already scattered elsewhere in this guide: matching hanbok rental at the palaces and the padlocks at N Seoul Tower (both Seoul), and a Han River sunset picnic at Yeouido (Seoul again). None of it needs planning in advance, all of it works as a same-day add-on.

CultureFriend group culture: it's not just for couples

Matching rings aren't exclusively a couple thing here. A ring worn on the index finger reads as a friendship signal rather than a romantic one, and it's genuinely common for close friend groups, not just pairs, to get matching sets done together, Ring University and Banji Cafe (both above) will happily do a group booking rather than just twos. Matching friendship bracelets are the cheaper, lower-commitment version of the same idea.

The bigger picture: a lot of what's already in this guide is built around groups by default rather than couples or solo travellers. A noraebang room, a PC bang session, an escape room (all in Entertainment & Nightlife) and the photo booth strip above are all a friend-group night out here, not a date, sized and priced for four or five people as standard, no adjustment needed if that's your travelling party.

CultureJjimjilbang: bathhouse & spa culture

A jjimjilbang is a 24-hour public bathhouse and sauna complex, and probably the single most-recommended cultural experience for visitors. Pay around ₩10,000-20,000 at the entrance counter and you get a wristband (locker key plus payment tracker), a uniform (T-shirt and shorts) and two small towels.

The wet-bath area (mogyoktang) is gender-separated and fully nude, not optional, shower at the cleaning stations before getting into any pool. The common areas beyond it, themed dry saunas, snack bars, sleeping rooms, are co-ed and everyone stays in uniform.

A full-body scrub service is a common paid add-on: an attendant with an exfoliating glove (an "Italy towel") scrubs you head to toe, a basic scrub runs roughly ₩25,000-40,000, premium versions with a milk rinse, oil massage and hair wash go up to ₩70,000.

Tattoos used to mean a firm no at many venues, but tattooing was legalised in Korea in 2025 and most large Seoul jjimjilbangs (SPAREX, Aquafield and similar) now permit them; smaller or more traditional venues, especially outside Seoul, may still restrict them, worth a quick check ahead if you're heavily tattooed.

Many visitors also use one as a genuine, cheap overnight stay, the heated sleeping rooms are a real budget accommodation option (see Before You Go), not just a novelty.

CultureOther things to avoid

  • Writing a Korean person's name in red ink: historically, the names of the deceased were recorded in red in family registers, so red-ink names became associated with death or wishing harm on someone. Many Koreans still find it genuinely rude even without consciously thinking through why, best avoided entirely, use any other colour.
  • The number 4: it sounds identical to the Sino-Korean word for "death" (사, sa), so some buildings label the fourth floor "F" instead of 4 on the lift. Not something you need to actively avoid as a tourist, but it explains an odd lift button if you notice one.
  • Pointing directly at people, especially with a single finger: comes across as confrontational. An open hand or a general gesture in someone's direction is the more polite way to indicate a person.
  • Blowing your nose at the table or in a public conversation: considered impolite in the same way it is in parts of East Asia more broadly, step away or use a toilet instead.

PracticalRules that actually get tourists in trouble

Most etiquette missteps just draw a look. These four can cost you money or worse, worth knowing before you're standing in the middle of one.

  • Jaywalking: even on a dead-quiet street at 2am with no cars in sight, locals wait for the green man. It's enforced, not just etiquette, on-the-spot fines run roughly ₩20,000 for crossing against the light at a crossing, up to ₩80,000 for crossing away from one entirely (about £10-40).
  • Smoking and vaping: most city streets, subway exits and parks in Seoul, Busan and Jeju are designated no-smoking zones, and since April 2026 vapes are treated identically to cigarettes under the law. Light up outside a designated smoking booth (an enclosed booth or a marked street box) and it's an instant ₩100,000 fine (about £50), and it's ordinary members of the public reporting it as often as patrols catching it.
  • Photographing strangers: Korea's privacy law is stricter than the UK's about identifiable faces in photos and video. Fine for a candid street shot; if you're posting it publicly, blur out anyone whose face is clearly recognisable and didn't agree to be in it.
  • Leaving a scathing review: unlike the UK, truth isn't an automatic defence against defamation here. Under Article 307 of the Criminal Act, damaging someone's reputation or business, even with entirely true facts, can be a criminal matter unless it's clearly in the public interest, and the online version of the law carries harsher penalties still. Worth remembering before naming and shaming a restaurant or hotel while you're still in the country.

CultureReligious observances

Korea has high religious freedom and no state religion, public schools don't teach religion, though private ones can. Roughly half the population (about 51%) has no religion, around 31% is Christian (split fairly evenly between Protestant and Catholic), and about 16% Buddhist.

What that produces day to day: temples and churches sit genuinely side by side, red neon crosses are as much a part of the night skyline as lit-up temple eaves. Both Christmas and Buddha's Birthday are national public holidays, a deliberate nod to that religious mix rather than a historical accident.

Buddha's Birthday (Seokga Tansinil) falls on 24 May 2026, and the Lotus Lantern Festival around it (main parade the Saturday before) fills central Seoul with thousands of lit paper lanterns, worth timing a visit around if the dates line up. Visiting either a temple or a church as a tourist is generally fine if you're quiet and dress modestly, the same guidance as the palaces applies.

Culture"Red-letter day" and Korea's own calendar of days

A quick clarification, since it's easy to conflate two different things: "red-letter day" is actually an English idiom, old church calendars marked holy days and holidays in red ink, and it's since come to mean any noteworthy date. Korean calendars follow a similar visual convention of their own, Sundays and public holidays are typically printed in red, Saturdays often in blue.

What Korea does have that's genuinely distinctive is a whole running calendar of "14th of the month" couple days:

Valentine's Day (14 February): women give men chocolate.

White Day (14 March): men reciprocate with sweets, "white" for purity, the custom actually originated in Japan and caught on here.

Black Day (14 April): for anyone who got nothing on either date, they mark it by eating jajangmyeon (black bean noodles) together.

Pepero Day (11 November) is the best-known day outside Korea, and it isn't part of the 14th-of-the-month set: friends and couples swap Pepero, a chocolate-dipped biscuit stick, because 11/11 written out looks like four sticks stood in a row. It's genuinely mainstream, not a K-pop fan thing, though its official status traces to Lotte (the maker of Pepero) formally naming the day in 1997 rather than to any older tradition, marketing that worked so well it became a real fixture of the calendar.

It continues on through the year in steadily more obscure, mostly commercial versions. Mostly a bit of fun rather than deep tradition, but it turns up constantly in K-dramas, so it's genuinely useful cultural context rather than trivia.

Beyond these fixed dates, festivals, concerts and local celebrations vary enormously by season and by city, see "Festivals worth timing a visit around" just below for a curated seasonal list, and the K-Pop tab for concert-specific pointers.

CultureFestivals worth timing a visit around

Korea's festival calendar moves with the seasons and shifts by a week or two most years, worth checking exact dates closer to your trip rather than locking plans to what's written here. A few worth building a visit around if the timing lines up:

Jeju Fire Festival (Saebyeol Oreum, Aewol, early-to-mid March): the same oreum covered in the Jeju tab, lit up for one night, the island's biggest deliberate burn, main event on the closing weekend.

Jinhae Gunhangje (Changwon, near Busan, late March into early April): Korea's biggest cherry blossom festival, ten days built around roughly 360,000 trees, an easy detour from a Busan or Gyeongju stop.

Jeonju International Film Festival (late April into May): if you're already in the Hanok Village for the food and the hanbok, this fills the town with screenings and a proper festival atmosphere on top.

Jeju Food & Wine Festival (Jeju Shinhwa World, late May into June): the island's leading food festival, chefs and producers from across Jeju.

Boryeong Mud Festival (Daecheon Beach, late July into August): the mud-wrestling festival most visitors have already heard of by name, plus K-pop concerts and a drone show most years, worth the day trip even though Boryeong sits outside this guide's core stops.

Tamna Cultural Festival (Jeju-wide, early October): the island's biggest folk festival, villages competing in traditional contests across Jeju.

Busan International Film Festival (BIFF, October): Asia's biggest film festival, red-carpet premieres and outdoor screenings along Haeundae and Nampo-dong.

Buddha's Birthday and the Lotus Lantern Festival are covered separately above under Religious observances, they're fixed enough to date precisely rather than needing the "check nearer the time" caveat.

CulturePhoto booth culture: 인생네컷 (insaeng-necut)

Self-service photo booths, 인생네컷 or "life four-cut", are everywhere in nightlife and shopping districts, Hongdae, Myeongdong and around Konkuk University especially, and taking a strip with friends at the end of a night out is close to a national ritual rather than a tourist novelty.

A set of four poses runs roughly ₩4,000-10,000. Most booths let you pick a background and frame style on a touchscreen before shooting through a countdown. The bigger chains (Life Four Cuts, Photoism, Haru Film) stock props, costumes and headbands, and some have a second, better-lit room for a more polished set at a higher price.

Photos print instantly, and most machines also text or airdrop a digital copy, worth having since the paper strip fades over time (glossy laminate finishes cost a little more and last properly). Cash avoids any card-reader faff, not every machine takes cards.

CultureRainy day ritual: pajeon & makgeolli

When it rains, Koreans have a running joke, and a genuine habit, of heading out for pajeon (a savoury green onion pancake, often with seafood mixed in) and makgeolli (a milky, lightly sparkling rice wine, usually ladled out from a kettle). The popular explanation is that the sizzle of batter hitting a hot pan mirrors the sound of rain on the ground, folk wisdom or just a nice story, but the pairing itself is real and widespread.

Older, no-frills tent bars and small alley restaurants tend to do it better than polished restaurants, look for a hotplate and regulars already in. A pajeon big enough to share runs roughly ₩12,000-18,000, a kettle of makgeolli ₩6,000-9,000, ordered together rather than one after the other.

Worth keeping in your back pocket: if a downpour catches you mid-sightseeing, this is the answer, not just standing under an awning waiting it out.

CultureKorean age got simpler in 2023

Korea used to run three different age systems at once: "Korean age" (a baby is 1 at birth, everyone gains a year together on New Year's Day), a separate "counting age" used for drinking and smoking laws, and the international system doctors already used. Since 28 June 2023, a new law made international age the single legal standard, so most Koreans are now officially one or two years younger than they'd have said a few years earlier. Older relatives may still quote Korean age out of habit, so don't be surprised if you hear a number that doesn't match someone's passport.

CultureBlood type: Korea's other horoscope

Ask someone's blood type in Korea and you might get a little more than medical information back. Blood type personality theory, imported from Japan in the 1970s, treats A, B, O and AB as rough personality profiles the way Western culture treats star signs, and a Gallup Korea poll found roughly six in ten adults still put some stock in it. It's mostly taken lightly these days (TV shows that leaned on it too heavily were pulled from 2008 onward after complaints it fed real discrimination), but it comes up often enough in K-dramas and casual conversation that it's worth recognising when it does.

The deck fencing is covered in thousands of padlocks, couples and visitors write a message and lock it to the railing, a tradition often called the "locks of love". It's also a recurring K-drama filming location, see K-Drama Spots for specific scenes.

CultureHanbok rental: free palace entry

Wear a rented hanbok and entry to Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung and Gyeonghuigung palaces is free (a standard adult ticket is only around ₩3,000 otherwise, so this is more about the experience than the saving).

Rental shops cluster around Gyeongbokgung Station and the palace's main entrance: a standard 4-hour rental runs roughly ₩15,000-25,000, full-day ₩25,000-30,000, premium/designer sets and couple sets cost more, hair styling is usually a small add-on. Most shops include free locker storage for your own clothes. Worth doing on a clear day since the photos are the whole point.

FoodMyeongdong

Seoul's main shopping district doubles as a street food strip, tteokbokki, fish cake skewers, hotteok in colder months. Central and easy to reach, good first stop.

CultureIkseondong

A pocket of small hanok houses just off Jongno, converted into cafés, dessert shops, natural wine bars and boutiques while keeping their traditional tiled roofs and tiny courtyards. Best in the early evening, once the lantern-lit signs and warm interior lighting come on, the alleys get genuinely photogenic rather than just pretty.

Worth wandering rather than following a fixed list, expect a rotating mix of pancake and soufflé cafés, a gallery-café hybrid or two, and a scattering of hanok wine bars sourcing from small Korean and European producers. Closest station is Jongno 3-ga (Lines 1, 3 and 5), a five-minute walk.

CultureHongdae

University-area energy: live music, indie cafés, late-night crowd. Best in the evening rather than the morning.

SightGangnam

Modern Seoul: upscale shopping, fine dining, nightlife. Worth a visit for contrast with the old town, even if you're not there to shop.

FoodGwangjang Market

Seoul's oldest traditional market. Try bindaetteok (mung bean pancake), mayak gimbap (small sesame rice rolls) and yukhoe (seasoned raw beef) from stalls some families have run for decades. If it feels too touristy, Mangwon Market is the local-leaning alternative.

PracticalGetting around

The subway is extensive, cheap and in English as well as Korean. Tap in with T-money. KakaoMap or Naver Map will route you accurately, Kakao T covers anything the subway doesn't.

Hidden GemMajang-dong Meat Market

Korea's largest meat market, modern, clean facilities, hanwoo beef (Korea's native cattle, marbling on par with the best wagyu) is the speciality.

Pick your cut at street level, then the shop sends you upstairs to a restaurant in the same building to grill it yourselves, a charcoal-grilling fee of around ₩7,000 a head on top of what you paid for the meat. A proper local food experience, not a tourist recreation of one.

Hidden GemYangnyeongsi Herbal Medicine Market

Korea's largest traditional medicine market, over 800 hanbang (traditional Korean medicine) shops and clinics clustered around Jegi-dong Station, Line 1 Exit 2.

The Seoul K-Medi Center inside runs interactive exhibits on the history and science behind hanbang, plus hands-on and personalised wellness programmes if you want to actually try something rather than just look. Genuinely sensory, ginseng, dried herbs and roots by the sackful, and a completely different pace to the rest of the city.

SightCaribbean Bay (Everland, Yongin)

One of Asia's biggest waterparks, indoor and outdoor sections so it partly runs year-round, about an hour from Seoul next to Everland theme park. The Mega Storm slide drops from 37m. In summer 2026 (3 July-17 August) it doubles as an open-air music festival with K-Pop and EDM stages, worth timing a visit around if that appeals, or avoiding if you'd rather have it quieter.

SightVivaldi Park Ocean World (Hongcheon)

The other big one, roughly the size of 14 football pitches, holds up to 18,000 people. About 1.5-2 hours from Seoul in Gangwon-do, part of a larger resort with ski slopes and golf alongside it. A full day out rather than a quick add-on, factor in the travel time.

SightWhat's included

Standard tours cover the Dora Observatory and the Third Tunnel (a tunnel dug toward Seoul, discovered in 1978).

Some include the JSA (Joint Security Area) at Panmunjom, the only spot where North and South Korean soldiers stand face to face, described by many as more affecting in person than expected.

🏛️ Gyeongju 2 min read

The old Silla capital, dense with UNESCO sites. Reachable by KTX from Seoul (to Singyeongju station) or as a stop between Seoul and Busan.

Daereungwon Tumuli, the royal burial mounds. Photo via Unsplash.

SightBulguksa Temple & Seokguram Grotto

Both UNESCO World Heritage sites and the headline reason to come.

Bulguksa is the temple complex.

Seokguram is a hillside grotto shrine with a granite Buddha, connected to Bulguksa by a short bus or taxi ride.

SightCheomseongdae Observatory

The oldest surviving astronomical observatory in Asia, built under Queen Seondeok. Small, striking, easy to combine with a walk through the tomb complex nearby.

SightDaereungwon Tomb Complex

Grassy burial mounds of Silla royalty right in the middle of the city, including Cheonmachong, one of the few tombs you can walk inside.

SightDonggung Palace & Wolji Pond

A Silla-era palace and pond complex built as a secondary palace and banquet hall, now Gyeongju's best-known night sight. The reconstructed pavilions sit right at the water's edge, and their reflection in Wolji Pond is the reason people come after dark rather than during the day.

Illuminated nightly, it's a short walk from Cheomseongdae Observatory and easy to combine with an evening stroll through the tomb complex once the heat of the day has passed.

Hidden GemGyerim Forest night walk

A thick, centuries-old forest of zelkova and willow trees right in central Gyeongju, its own quiet legend attached (said to be where the Silla-era Kim clan's founder was discovered as an infant). Free to enter, small enough to walk in 15-20 minutes.

After dark the whole route lights up in purple, orange and warm yellow, past Cheomseongdae Observatory and the illuminated burial mounds, finishing with a five-minute walk on to Woljeonggyo Bridge for the nightscape view. String it together with Donggung Palace & Wolji Pond (above) for one continuous evening walk rather than treating them as separate stops.

Hidden GemAndong Hahoe Folk Village

A preserved clan village about an hour from Gyeongju, UNESCO-listed, still genuinely lived in rather than reconstructed for visitors. Confucian academies and folk traditions, including the Hahoe mask dance, carry on as living practice, not a museum piece.

Worth a half-day add-on if you're already spending a few days around Gyeongju, rather than a dedicated trip in its own right.

Hidden GemBoseong Green Tea Fields

Terraced emerald hills in South Jeolla province, a different scale and setting from Jeju's O'Sulloc plantation (see the Jeju tab), with a quiet tea-tasting session available among the rows.

Worth being honest about the geography though: it's on the opposite side of the country from Gyeongju, a standalone day rather than a side trip, better folded into a broader Jeolla-region itinerary than tacked onto this leg.

PracticalGetting there & around

KTX to Singyeongju is the fastest option from Seoul or Busan. A day-tour package (KTX plus guided Bulguksa/Seokguram visit and lunch) simplifies logistics if you'd rather not self-navigate the bus routes to the sites.

🌊 Busan 2 min read

Korea's second city, coastal, more laid-back than Seoul. 2 nights is enough to hit the highlights.

Haeundae Beach, where the skyline meets the sand. Photo via Unsplash.

Schematic layout, not to scale. Gamcheon and Jagalchi are about 15 minutes apart, easy to pair in one day.

SightHaeundae Beach

Korea's most famous beach, a 1.5km crescent backed by high-rises. More of a city-beach vibe than a quiet escape, good for an afternoon.

Hidden GemHaeundae Blueline Sky Capsule

A retro, brightly coloured four-seat private cabin running along an elevated monorail at walking pace (about 5km/h) along the cliffside near Haeundae, unobstructed panoramic ocean views the whole way.

Route: book the Mipo to Cheongsapo direction specifically, that keeps your capsule on the outer track closest to the ocean. The reverse direction runs on the inner track, with the view partly blocked by passing cabins.

Timing: aim for a slot roughly 30 minutes before sunset for the best light as the sun drops into the sea.

Booking: tickets sell out weeks in advance. Book online via the official Haeundae Blueline Park site the moment your dates are confirmed, walk-up tickets on weekends are almost non-existent.

CultureGamcheon Culture Village

Pastel houses terraced up a hillside, once a refugee settlement, now murals, small galleries and cafés. Arrive before 10am on a weekday for good light and fewer crowds. Pick up the stamp-trail map at the entrance and wander off the main path.

FoodJagalchi Fish Market

Korea's largest seafood market. Pick a live fish downstairs, have it prepared sashimi-style and eat upstairs. Near Jagalchi and Nampo stations.

Hidden GemChoryang Milmyeon Lane

The dish: milmyeon, a cold wheat noodle served in an icy, savoury broth, born after the Korean War when wheat flour from aid supplies replaced the traditional buckwheat.

The experience: tucked into an unassuming neighbourhood behind Busan Station, a quieter, more affordable slice of local food history well away from the beach crowds.

PracticalGetting there

KTX from Seoul is around 2.5 hours; from Gyeongju it's a short hop on the same line. Busan's own subway covers the city centre well.

🏯 Jeonju 2 min read

Korea's UNESCO City of Gastronomy and the historic home of hanbok craftsmanship. Usually a standalone trip from Seoul, or added onto the Gyeongju/Busan leg if you have an extra day or two.

Jeonju Hanok Village from above. Photo via Unsplash.

SightJeonju Hanok Village

One of Korea's most complete traditional hanok neighbourhoods, on a similar scale to Bukchon but with a stronger food-and-craft identity. The core sights, walkable in a day:

Gyeonggijeon Shrine: houses a portrait of Yi Seong-gye, the first Joseon king.

Jeondong Cathedral.

Omokdae Pavilion: panoramic village views.

CultureHanbok, properly

Jeonju is where Korean hanbok craftsmanship actually comes from, so rental here (and the wider hanbok culture around the village) has more depth to it than the Seoul palace version, worth doing here specifically if the craft itself is what interests you rather than just the photo.

CultureHanji papermaking: Jeonju's other craft

Alongside hanbok, Jeonju is the historic home of hanji, paper hand-made from mulberry bark, strong enough to be woven, lacquered or used as flooring in traditional buildings, not just written on. The Jeonju Hanji Museum and the nearby Hanji Industry Support Center run short hands-on sessions, making your own sheet, a lamp, or a paper doll, an easy pairing with a Hanok Village day since it's a five-to-ten-minute taxi from the core sights.

If Jeonju isn't on the itinerary, Seoul's Bukchon studios (Gahoe-dong Hanji Studio and similar) offer the same craft closer to the city, shorter sessions of an hour or two covering hanji lamps, dolls or frames.

FoodJeonju bibimbap & the Bibimbap and Food Alley

This is the dish's spiritual home, Jeonju-style bibimbap uses more ingredients, better rice and a raw egg yolk on top, genuinely different from Seoul's version.

The Bibimbap and Food Alley in the hanok village is full of specialists, alongside kongnamul gukbap (bean sprout soup) stalls and street snacks.

Nambu Market's night market is worth it if you're staying over.

PracticalGetting there & how long to stay

KTX from Seoul Station to Jeonju takes about 1hr50 (around ₩27,600); the budget option is the express bus from Seoul's Honam Express Bus Terminal, 2.5-3 hours from around ₩10,800, running almost hourly.

From Jeonju Station, a taxi to the Hanok Village is ₩5,000-7,000, about 10 minutes. One day covers the core sights and a meal; two days lets you catch the early-morning atmosphere before the crowds and the Nambu Market night market.

🌋 Jeju Island 8 min read

Volcanic island off the south coast, fly-only from the mainland (about an hour from Seoul or Busan). 3-4 nights lets you cover east, west and the centre without rushing.

Jeju's volcanic coastline, the terrain behind most of the island's headline sights. Photo via Unsplash.

Schematic layout, not to scale. The island is compact enough that most sights are 40-90 minutes apart by car.

SightSeongsan Ilchulbong (Sunrise Peak)

A 180m volcanic tuff cone, UNESCO-listed, famous for sunrise views. The climb to the top takes about 30 minutes and suits most fitness levels.

SightHallasan

South Korea's tallest mountain, a national park and biosphere reserve at the island's centre. Full summit hikes run 8+ hours round trip, there are shorter trail options if that's too much for the day.

SightUdo Island

A short ferry from near Seongsan Ilchulbong. Small, easy to circle by bike or scooter in a few hours, beaches, coastal viewpoints, quirky cafés.

SightSeogwipo: waterfalls & coastal cliffs

Jeju's south-coast city and the base for most of the island's headline natural sights, close enough together for one loop.

Cheonjiyeon Falls ("God's Pond") drops through a forested gorge into a pool, no swimming allowed, but an easy, well-paved walk from the car park and worth doing even briefly.

Jusangjeolli Cliffs, hexagonal basalt columns formed where an old Hallasan lava flow met the sea and cooled, look almost too geometric to be natural.

Oedolgae Rock, a lone 20m volcanic sea stack just offshore, anchors a coastal walking trail through pine forest, budget an hour or two if you want to walk it properly rather than just photograph it from the car park.

FoodSeogwipo Maeil Olle Market

Open since 1960, Seogwipo's own big market, and a genuine alternative to Jeju City's Dongmun if you're based south. Open daily 7am-9pm, with the food side really coming alive in the evening: around 20 stalls cooking up grilled eel, king crab, black pork steak and black pork yakisoba, alongside the island's seafood and hallabong citrus.

FoodJeju City: Dongmun Market & Black Pork Street

Most visitors land here, Jeju International Airport is a 15-minute drive from the centre, and it's worth more than a pass-through if you've an evening free before flying out.

Dongmun Traditional Market, open since 1945, is the island's largest, by day it's produce, seafood and souvenirs, but from 6pm the Dongmun Night Market takes over with street food stalls and a proper crowd, torch-grilled black pork skewers are what to queue for.

Black Pork Street (Geonip-dong), a designated strip of 15-plus specialist restaurants about 10 minutes from the airport, is the sit-down version of the same speciality if a market stall isn't what you're after.

For a more refined, sit-down version of the same thing, Poongro Black in Andeok-myeon (west of Seogwipo) runs a dry-aged black pork tasting menu, its own "Butakase" omakase-style format, well worth the detour if you want the black pork experience done properly once rather than repeatedly at a market stall.

If it's not black pork you're after, Subokgangnyeong near the Provincial Office does its own standout version of tteokgalbi (minced short-rib patties), served in oversized squares, black pork or hanwoo Korean beef. Made to order rather than pre-prepped, so call ahead to reserve.

SightManjanggul Cave & lava tubes

Part of the island's volcanic heritage, walkable lava tube caves. Good option if the weather turns.

Hidden GemGimnyeong Beach & its fishing village

A small white-sand beach over lava rock on the northeast coast, close enough to Manjanggul (above) to pair the two in one loop. Walk along the black lava rock and clear water to Gimnyeong Port, a working fishing village where local haenyeo still run a diving programme, and you're on part of a UNESCO Global Geopark geological trail the whole way, so it's a proper walk with something to read as well as a stretch of sand. Budget 2-4 hours to do it without rushing.

SightOreums: Jeju's volcanic cones

Oreum is Jeju dialect for a parasitic volcanic cone, a small satellite crater scattered around Hallasan rather than the main peak itself. There are over 360 of them across the island, formed by the same eruptions that shaped Jeju roughly two million years ago, and climbing one is one of the most distinctly Jeju things you can do, quieter and far less crowded than the headline sights.

Most are free to enter, unstaffed and open any time, no booking needed. Best light is early morning or sunset, and if you're here between September and November, several are genuinely famous for silver grass (eok-sae) rippling across their slopes.

Yongnuni Oreum (near Seongsan): named for its dragon-like shape, a gentle 30-minute climb with sunrise and sunset views plus a clear look at Seongsan Ilchulbong itself, an easy add-on if you're already out that way.

Saebyeol Oreum (near Aewol, close to Dochidol Ranch below): one of the most popular for its autumn silver grass and sunset views over Hallasan and Biyangdo Island, a steeper but short climb.

Sara Oreum (off the Seongpanak trail, see Hallasan above): the island's highest oreum, and unusually has its own crater lake at the summit. Reachable as part of a Hallasan hike via Seongpanak, roughly three hours in, worth knowing about before you commit to that trail if you'd like to see it.

Wear proper shoes: wooden-deck staircases and open grassland both turn slippery after rain, and there's rarely any shelter once you're on the slope.

Hidden GemOff-road oreum truck tours

If climbing an oreum on foot isn't the point for you, a guided off-road 4WD tour covers similar volcanic terrain from the driver's seat instead: horse paths, uphill and downhill trail sections, Gotjawal forest and open meadow where wild horses and deer roam, with a professional off-road driver doing the actual driving. Runs about an hour, a good option if you want the landscape without the climb, or as a change of pace from the hiking-heavy days elsewhere on this list.

SightCheonwangsa Temple & the Hallasan forest path

A quieter, less-visited route up Hallasan's eastern side, at the base of Gugugok ("ninety-nine valleys"). The temple sits below dense forest and dramatic rock, with the mountain's only waterfall and a mineral spring a short walk along the valley, plus a nearby forest path into Cheona Valley that's genuinely one of the better autumn foliage spots on the island. A good pick if the main Hallasan trails (above) feel too crowded or too long for the day you've got.

SightO'Sulloc Tea Museum & green tea fields

Jeju's volcanic soil genuinely suits green tea, and O'Sulloc's museum and surrounding plantation near Seogwipo is the place to see it: emerald tea fields, a tea culture exhibition, and a café serving green tea in every form. No booking needed just to walk around.

For something more structured, the museum's own guided tea ceremony adds a stroll through the fields and a proper tasting session, book ahead on the museum's website rather than turning up expecting a slot.

CultureHaenyeo: Jeju's free-diving women

The haenyeo are Jeju's traditional female free-divers, some still working into their 70s and 80s, who dive without oxygen tanks to gather abalone, sea urchin and shellfish, holding their breath for roughly a minute per dive across shifts of several hours. UNESCO inscribed the culture of the haenyeo on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016.

Divers are organised by experience (hagun, junggun and sanggun, junior to senior) and gather afterwards at a bulteok, a communal fire by the shore, a tradition UNESCO specifically noted for showing both Jeju's distinct identity and a real model of women's cooperation and empowerment. Numbers have fallen sharply, from a peak of around 23,000 to roughly 4,300 today, as fewer young women take it up.

The Jeju Haenyeo Museum (Haenyeo Museum, in Gujwa-eup on the island's northeast side) is the place to see the history and equipment up close, and several coastal villages, including around Seongsan, have haenyeo-run seafood restaurants and stalls selling the day's catch straight off the boat.

SightDochidol Ranch: alpacas up close

A working alpaca farm in Aewol, on the island's north coast, where alpacas roam a fairly open field rather than pens, alongside ponies, sheep, goats and rabbits. Feeding is part of the entry, hand a handful of hay to an alpaca and it'll come straight over, and the on-site Experience House lends out ponchos (the traditional Peruvian garment, since that's where alpacas actually come from) free for photos.

SightJeju Leports Land: go-karts, zipline, paintball

The island's biggest recreation complex and Korea's largest go-kart centre, all on one site: go-karting, a zipline, a water sled and paintball. For thrill-seekers this is the on-par answer to the water parks already in this guide, one stop rather than several bookings, and it doesn't need the mainland detour Everland or Lotte World would.

CultureJeju horse riding: the native pony

Jeju has its own distinct horse breed, the Jeju pony (jorangmal), small, sturdy and bred for the island's terrain rather than a generic riding-school animal. Jeju Horse Park runs graded track lengths from a short 1.2km loop up to a 13km outing, while Steel Flower Ranch and similar operators offer beach riding specifically, worth seeking out over an inland arena if the coastline is the whole point for you. Beginners and children are well catered for, instructors walk alongside for the shorter courses.

FoodWhat to eat

  • Heuk-dwaeji: Jeju black pork, grilled.
  • Galchi-jorim: braised hairtail.
  • Abalone porridge.
  • Sea urchin soup.
  • Fresh sashimi.
  • Tangerine everything: juice, desserts, even soft serve. Jeju's citrus is a genuine local speciality, not just a gift-shop gimmick.

Hidden GemA few Jeju cafés worth the detour

Slowboat Atelier (Aewol): a two-storey atelier-style café built to resemble a tangerine warehouse, camera-lens windows, sea views, cats, and pastries from Abebe Bakery.

lavarr (Seogwipo): a three-storey former hot spring bath turned café, each floor a different interior, well-reviewed for its salted nut latte.

Muroi (Andeok-myeon): black-exteriored, gallery-like, dark concrete against a lush garden, known for tiramisu served in a pot.

Cafe Gyulkkot Darak (Seogwipo): a tangerine-themed dessert café in a 1978 stone building surrounded by a working orchard.

Maeum Eon (Jeju City): a small hanok tucked into a market alley, easy to walk straight past, cosy and traditional once you find the door.

PracticalGetting around

A rental car roughly halves your travel time between sights and is the practical choice given how spread out they are.

You'll need your passport, UK driving licence, and a physical International Driving Permit (1968 Vienna Convention version) arranged before you fly, it isn't issued on the island. Buses exist but run infrequently and slowly between major sights, workable if you're not in a hurry, frustrating if you are.

🧭 Hidden Coastal Gems 2 min read

Two quiet coastal towns worth the detour once the main circuit (Busan, Jeju) feels like it's had enough crowds. Both take more effort to reach than anything else in this guide, and reward it precisely because most visitors don't bother.

Korea's quieter coastline, away from the main circuit. Photo via Unsplash.

Hidden GemYangyang: Surf Culture & Coastal Pine Forests

The vibe: a once-sleepy fishing area on Gangwon-do's east coast that's quietly become Korea's eco-surf capital, without losing the raw, uncrowded feel that made it worth visiting in the first place.

What to see: Surfyy Beach, wide, windswept sand with a genuine surf-town scene (lessons, board rental, beach cafés), and Hajodae Pavilion, perched on a jagged cliff ringed by old pine trees, one of the quieter viewpoints on this coast.

This is a slow stop rather than a sightseeing one, better suited to a day or two tacked onto an east-coast leg than a tick-box visit. If surf culture and quiet pine-backed coastline don't appeal, it's an easy one to skip.

Getting there: intercity bus from Seoul to the Yangyang Intercity Bus Terminal, then the local Hajodae-bound loop bus toward the beach. Rural bus schedules thin out fast and English signage is rare this far out, see the Getting Around tab before relying on the last bus back.

Hidden GemGuryongpo: Historic Port & Dramatic Cliffs

The vibe: a quiet, beautifully preserved harbour town on the peninsula's eastern tail near Pohang, industrial-meets-historic rather than polished for visitors.

What to see: the Guryongpo Japanese Houses Street, a preserved 1920s alleyway of dark wooden colonial-era architecture, then a short local bus out to Homigot Sunrise Square, home to the famous bronze hand sculpture rising straight out of the sea.

Pairs naturally with a Gyeongju trip since Pohang is the closest transport hub, worth a half-day detour rather than a dedicated trip of its own.

Getting there: KTX to Pohang Station, then Bus 900 or 9000 from the transfer plaza, a scenic, bumpy 40-minute coastal ride that drops you right in front of the Japanese Houses Street.

🎭 Arts & Heritage 3 min read

For anyone here for the culture and craft as much as the sightseeing: traditional performance, art, hanbok heritage, and gardens, the well-known ones and a few quieter ones locals point people to. Everything below is publicly documented, not insider secrets, just less on the standard tourist track.

Huwon, the Secret Garden. Photo via Unsplash.

SightTraditional performances

The Royal Guard Changing Ceremony at Gyeongbokgung's Gwanghwamun Gate runs daily at 10am and 2pm, free, and re-enacts 600 years of actual palace security practice.

Nanta is Seoul's best-known ticketed show, a non-verbal percussion performance set in a kitchen, blending samulnori rhythm with comedy (Myeongdong/Hongdae venues).

The National Jeongdong Theater's GWANGDAE repertoire covers pansori, samulnori and royal court dance with English subtitles.

The National Gugak Center's Saturday afternoon performance (3pm, most weeks) is aimed specifically at introducing traditional Korean music and dance to foreign visitors, a good low-key way in if you've never seen gugak before.

SightArt museums & galleries

The Leeum Museum of Art (Hannam-dong, Samsung-owned) is the standout: three buildings by Mario Botta, Jean Nouvel and Rem Koolhaas, one wing of traditional Korean art including national treasures, the other contemporary and international.

The National Museum of Korea is the big historical collection if you want the full sweep.

The Seoul Museum of Art (SeMA) covers local and international contemporary work.

Hidden GemGalleries off the main path

Samcheongdong and Insadong, both walkable from Bukchon, hide a genuine gallery scene behind the main tourist strip.

Gallery Hyundai and the Daelim Museum (photography and design, smaller and quieter than the big institutions) are the two most worth seeking out deliberately rather than stumbling into.

CultureHanbok, beyond the rental photo

If hanbok is a genuine interest rather than a one-off photo, Jeonju (its own tab in this guide) is where the craft actually comes from, hanbok culture there runs deeper than the Seoul palace rental shops. In Seoul itself, Insadong and Bukchon have proper tailoring ateliers doing custom and traditional-technique hanbok work, worth a look even just to see the craft up close.

CultureIcheon Ceramics Village: celadon and buncheong, hands-on

Icheon, about an hour southeast of Seoul, is a UNESCO City of Crafts and Folk Art built around dozens of working kilns and studios producing celadon (cheongja) and buncheong ware. Walk in and you'll see potters actually throwing and glazing, not a staged demonstration, and most studios run hands-on sessions where you shape and glaze your own piece, fired and ready to collect or ship on afterwards.

This is the genuine equivalent of a jade or silk factory visit elsewhere in Asia: watch the craft, try it yourself, buy directly from the person who made it rather than a shop mark-up. Worth a full day trip from Seoul rather than trying to bolt it onto something else.

SightHuwon, the Secret Garden (Changdeokgung)

A UNESCO-listed rear garden behind Changdeokgung Palace, pavilions, ponds and old trees laid out for the royal family's private use. It's ticketed separately from the main palace and only accessible on a timed guided tour with limited daily capacity, book ahead rather than turning up expecting to walk straight in.

Hidden GemQuieter parks, gardens & temples (when you need a breather)

Seoul Forest blends parkland with art installations and a small deer enclosure, popular with locals but well below palace-level tourist traffic.

Seoul Botanic Park (opened 2019, in Magok) is quiet most of the time despite a genuinely large lake and wetland area, one of the least-visited proper sights in the city.

Namsan Outdoor Botanical Garden, tucked into the Namsan hillside, is a quiet run of seasonal flowers and walking paths that feels far removed from the cable car queues nearby.

Gilsangsa Temple, in Seongbuk-dong (see below), is a genuinely tranquil, meditation-focused temple, a gentler first temple visit than Jogyesa or Bongeunsa.

Bongwonsa Temple is a quieter alternative to Jogyesa and Bongeunsa, home to the striking Three Thousand Buddhas Hall, and rarely on a first-time itinerary.

Buam-dong, tucked against the northern mountains, is a quiet café-and-viewpoint neighbourhood many visitors never reach.

Seongbukdong pairs traditional hanok streets with a genuine gallery-and-walking-trail scene, worth an afternoon if Bukchon felt too crowded to properly enjoy.

For a quieter coastal alternative to Busan or Jeju's main circuits, see Yangyang and Guryongpo in the Hidden Coastal Gems tab.

SightDepartment stores worth it for the experience

The Hyundai Seoul (Yeouido) is less a shop and more an attraction: a 3,300m² indoor park with real grass and around 30 real trees on the fifth floor, plus a 12-metre indoor waterfall cascading through the main atrium. Genuinely worth visiting even with zero shopping intent.

SightIconic shopping streets

Garosu-gil in Gangnam is the boutique, tree-lined equivalent for higher-end browsing.

The street around Ewha Womans University has younger, more affordable fashion aimed at students.

Dongdaemun runs enormous wholesale fashion complexes, some genuinely open 24 hours, alongside the Zaha Hadid-designed Dongdaemun Design Plaza (DDP), which hosts Seoul Fashion Week and rotating design exhibitions even if wholesale fashion isn't your thing.

Hidden GemSeongsu-dong & Yeonnam-dong: the design districts

Seongsu-dong, nicknamed "the Brooklyn of Seoul", is a former shoe-factory and printing district turned warehouse-cafe and indie-fashion hub, brand pop-ups rotate every 2-4 weeks so it never looks the same twice (also covered in K-Pop for its SM Entertainment building).

Yeonnam-dong, just off Hongdae, has a quieter, more residential indie boutique scene, Gentle Monster's flagship store (striking minimalist eyewear/fashion) is the best-known anchor.

Both reward wandering more than a fixed itinerary.

Dongmyo Flea Market, around 600 stalls near Dongmyo Shrine, is Seoul's biggest vintage/secondhand market, genuinely popular with locals for vintage clothing and odd finds, not really set up for tourists.

The nearby Seoul Folk Flea Market (Sinseol-dong Station) leans further into antiques, retro Korean collectibles and true secondhand goods, open 10am-7pm.

Both are the closest thing to a proper rummage in this guide.

Olive Young is the dominant K-beauty retail chain, several branches within a few streets of each other in Myeongdong or Hongdae alone, plus outposts in Incheon Airport. It's the one-stop shop for skincare, make-up and the sheet masks Korea is famous for, usually far cheaper here than the same brands sold abroad.

🎮 Entertainment & Nightlife 4 min read

From a full night out to a quiet PC bang and a private karaoke room, Korea's entertainment culture runs on more than just bars and clubs.

A neon-lit entertainment street after dark. Photo via Unsplash.

SightSports Monster

Sports Monster is Korea's largest indoor sports theme park, tucked inside Starfield malls in Goyang, Hanam, Daejeon and Anseong.

An unassuming entrance opens into a huge indoor activity park: rock climbing, high ropes, a flying fox zipline, a jumping tower, trampolines, archery, basketball/volleyball courts, darts, and a VR baseball pitching game, all under one roof. Entry runs from around ₩17,000-30,000 for one to two hours.

CultureNightlife Districts

Hongdae: young, indie, affordable, cover charges around ₩15,000, the default for students and a casual first night out.

Itaewon: Seoul's most international district by a distance, more English spoken here than anywhere else, craft cocktails and a genuinely mixed crowd (also the LGBTQ+ hub, see Emergency & Health).

Gangnam: the showpiece end, bottle service, tables from ₩300,000, dress to match.

Euljiro (nicknamed "Hipjiro"): industrial print shops by day, speakeasy-style wine and beer bars tucked behind old shopfronts by night, the trendiest option if Hongdae feels too young and Gangnam too flashy.

Jongno, next door, skews older and more local, the most authentically Korean late-night scene of the lot rather than one built around tourists or students.

CultureNoraebang: karaoke, properly

A noraebang ("song room") is a private soundproofed room your group rents by the hour, not a stage in front of strangers, the word made it into the Oxford English Dictionary in a December 2024 batch of Korean additions (alongside dalgona, hyung and tteokbokki). Cheap, everywhere, and open late, genuinely one of the best low-pressure nights out on offer, tambourines are provided, singing ability is optional.

CultureDrinking games

If a table starts playing one of these, you'll recognise it from every K-drama drinking scene:

Baskin Robbins 31: players take turns counting upward calling one, two or three numbers at once, whoever's forced to say "31" drinks.

369 (sam-yuk-gu): count upward together, but clap instead of saying any number containing a 3, 6 or 9 (double digits like 33 need two claps), the 30s are chaos, mess up and you drink.

Both are genuinely easy to join even with minimal Korean, the counting is the whole game.

Hidden GemPC bangs: gaming cafe culture

A PC bang (PC방) is a 24-hour gaming cafe, high-spec computers, food and drink ordered from your seat and delivered to your desk, over 18,000 of them nationwide, most densely in Seoul. Not just for hardcore gamers, cheap (a couple of pounds an hour), genuinely used socially, and an easy way to see a very ordinary slice of Korean daily life rather than a tourist-facing version of it.

SightEscape rooms

Korea takes escape rooms seriously, Seoul's scene is dense and well-reviewed, with a growing number of English-language rooms aimed specifically at foreign visitors, concentrated in Hongdae. A good rainy-afternoon or evening option that isn't a bar.

FoodHan River after dark: chimaek & the fountain bridge

Chimaek (chicken + maekju/beer) delivered straight to a picnic blanket by the Han River is a genuine slice of Korean life, not just a K-drama trope.

Yeouido, Ttukseom, Banpo and Mangwon parks have marked "Delivery Zones" (배달존), order from an app, a scooter rider finds your exact zone and calls you within 30-40 minutes. It isn't limited to chicken either, pizza, coffee and pretty much anything else on the app works the same way, see the Baemin entry in Before You Go for how this actually works for visitors.

Pair it with the Banpo Bridge Rainbow Fountain (April-October, several 20-minute shows nightly, typically 7:30pm, 8pm, 8:30pm and 9pm, check the current schedule), the world's longest bridge fountain, lit and choreographed after dark.

SightKBO baseball: the loudest cheap night out in Seoul

Korean baseball crowds sing, chant and dance for nine innings straight, led by cheerleaders and a team-specific chant book, it's as much a live show as a sports match, and one of the most-recommended genuine local nights out going.

Jamsil Baseball Stadium (Sports Complex Station, Line 2) is the historic home of rivals LG Twins and Doosan Bears, loud and old-school, worth catching before it's demolished at the end of the 2026 season. Gocheok Sky Dome, Korea's only domed stadium, is the rainy-day or midsummer alternative, home to the Kiwoom Heroes, tickets run 20-30% higher than open-air stadiums for the roof.

The season runs late March to September. Buy through Ticketlink Global or Interpark Global (both take foreign cards), tickets roughly ₩12,000-70,000 depending on seat, bring your passport.

PracticalIf you run out of medication

Korean pharmacies can't dispense most prescription medicines without a Korean doctor's prescription, even with your UK one in hand. 1339 or 1330 can point you to a walk-in clinic ("의원") to get a local prescription. Bringing a spare few days' supply, plus a copy of your original prescription, is the simplest way to avoid needing this at all.

PracticalBefore you travel: medical conditions & medication

Ordinary, non-controlled medication: bring up to a 3-month supply for personal use, in original packaging, with your prescription and ideally a doctor's letter stating your condition and listing what you're carrying.

Controlled substances are stricter than you'd expect: medicines containing controlled substances (this can include some common UK prescriptions such as certain ADHD medication, strong painkillers, and benzodiazepines) need advance approval from Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) before you travel, applied for online ahead of time.

CBD and THC products are effectively prohibited, don't bring them even with a UK prescription, including on a layover. If you're on any regular prescription, worth checking your specific medication against the MFDS rules a few weeks out rather than assuming it's fine.

PracticalSharps & medical waste (e.g. diabetes)

Bring enough travel-sized, puncture-resistant sharps containers for the whole trip. Used sharps are fine in hand luggage as long as they're in a proper sealed container.

For disposal, ask at a pharmacy, clinic or hospital, most will take a sealed sharps container off your hands, Papago's camera or a simple "이거 버려주세요" (igeo beoryeojuseyo, "please dispose of this") covers the language gap. Don't put loose sharps in general waste bins.

EmergencyIf something happens

Medical emergency: call 119, ambulance and interpretation are free.

Crime or theft: call 112, or find the nearest police box (파출소) or tourist police point, both common in central areas.

It's worth asking your hotel to write your accommodation's address in Korean on a card early in the trip, useful for taxis and for police if you ever need to explain where you're staying.

Anything serious enough to need consular help, arrest, lost passport, a major incident, contact the British Embassy.

EmergencyLost passport or belongings: the actual steps

South Korea runs LOST112 (lost112.go.kr), a nationwide digital lost-and-found registry, available in English, searchable by date, location and item type. Virtually everything handed in by bus drivers, restaurant staff or police ends up logged here, genuinely worth checking before assuming something's gone for good, especially somewhere rural, a dropped bag or wallet there is more likely to end up with the local bus terminal manager or nearest police box (파출소) than lost for good.

If it's your passport specifically:

1. File a report at the nearest police station or police box first, "여권을 분실했습니다" covers "I lost my passport". They'll issue an official Certificate of Lost Property, and you need this document before an emergency passport can be issued, so this step isn't optional.

2. If there's no English-speaking officer around, call 1330 (the tourist hotline listed above), a live translator joins the call with the police officer so the report goes through properly.

3. Emergency travel documents can only be issued in Seoul, where the British Embassy is (see above), not locally, so you'll need to travel back with the police certificate in hand.

4. If train or bus tickets were lost along with the passport, the ticket counter at the rural terminal can often look up your booking by name and phone number and print a replacement once you show them the certificate.

PracticalGeneral safety

Korea rates as one of the safer countries for travellers by most standard measures, Seoul and Busan both score well below London on standard crime indices, and walking alone at night in central areas is generally unremarkable. Ordinary street-smart precautions still apply.

One specific, less obvious risk worth knowing about: "molka", illegal hidden cameras occasionally found in public toilets, changing rooms and some budget motel rooms, if something looks off (a suspicious hole, reflective dot, or oddly placed object), it's a known enough issue that raising it with staff won't seem strange.

PracticalSolo & women's safety

South Korea is consistently rated a strong destination for solo and women travellers. Seoul's subway has women-only carriages at points during rush hour, well-lit streets, and extensive CCTV coverage.

Standard precautions (sticking to licensed taxis or Kakao T, keeping a copy of accommodation details on you) apply here as anywhere, but the baseline risk of street harassment or assault is genuinely lower than in much of Europe or North America.

PracticalLGBTQ+ travellers

Consensual same-sex relationships are legal in Korea, but same-sex marriage and civil partnerships aren't legally recognised, and anti-discrimination protections remain limited and contested.

In practice, physical violence or open harassment toward LGBTQ+ visitors is rare, the more common friction is occasional coldness or staring from older generations rather than confrontation. Itaewon is the country's best-known LGBTQ+ hub (bars and clubs around the area nicknamed "Homo Hill"), with a smaller, more traditional scene around Jongno.

Public affection guidance (see Culture & Etiquette) applies to any couple regardless, Korea's general conservatism about PDA isn't specific to LGBTQ+ visitors, though it's worth being aware attitudes can be more mixed outside Seoul's more cosmopolitan pockets.

PracticalAccessibility

Seoul's subway is wheelchair accessible at every station, most have two or more lifts (street-to-concourse and concourse-to-platform), and stations without a lift use a mechanical stairway platform lift instead. Trains have dedicated wheelchair spaces and a minimal platform gap.

Older sights are the weaker point: palaces and hanok villages often have uneven stone paths, high thresholds and steps that predate modern accessibility standards, worth checking a specific site's access notes if mobility is a concern.

🚄 Getting Around 7 min read

Pick two places and see the realistic ways to get between them, with rough time and price. Schedules and fares shift constantly, treat these as planning ballpark figures, not live bookable prices.

PracticalWho actually runs this, and where to book

Local subways have their own operators, Seoul Metro (Seoul Transportation Corporation) runs most of the Seoul subway, Busan Transportation Corporation runs Busan's, roughly the local equivalent of TfL. None of this matters day to day, though: KakaoMap, Naver Map and the Subway Korea/Tmoney app (all in Before You Go) already handle routing and fares, there's no separate operator app you need for riding the metro.

Intercity high-speed rail is different and isn't covered by any app mentioned so far. Korail is the national operator, it runs KTX and the regular/express trains, closest thing to a Network Rail equivalent. SR Corporation runs the separate SRT service (from Suseo Station rather than Seoul Station), a competitor on some of the same routes.

Booking either is its own step. Korail's Let's Korail site (letskorail.com) is the official option built for foreign cards and English, generally more reliable than the Korail app itself for card payments. SRT books separately at the SR site (etk.srail.kr) or its app, foreign card acceptance there is patchier. Both sell at station kiosks and ticket windows with English options too, and Klook and similar platforms (already mentioned elsewhere in this guide) list KTX/SRT tickets if a card gets rejected on the official site.

Worth knowing: KTX and SRT are due to become fully interconnected and freely switchable between stations from around mid-2026, with a single combined booking app expected by the end of the year, check whether that's landed by the time you travel, it may simplify all of this.

PracticalReading the subway: lines, colours & fares

Both Seoul's and Busan's systems are numbered and colour-coded, and every platform sign shows both, so you can navigate by colour alone without reading Korean.

Seoul: Line 1 is dark blue, Line 2 (the loop line, most useful for a first visit, it covers Hongdae, Myeongdong and Gangnam) is green, Line 3 is orange, Line 4 is sky blue, Line 5 is violet, plus several more numbered and suburban lines (AREX to the airport, Bundang, Sinbundang and others) as the network keeps growing.

Busan: a simpler four-line system, Line 1 orange, Line 2 green, Line 3 brown, Line 4 light blue, plus the separate Busan-Gimhae Light Rail (violet) and Donghae Line (blue) further out. A fifth line is expected to open in Busan in December 2026.

Fares: both cities charge a base fare (currently around ₩1,400 with T-money) with a small distance surcharge past the first 10km, and a free transfer between subway and bus if you tag out and back in within about 30 minutes, worth knowing before a multi-stop day.

For the actual live line map, station by station, search any station name in the Subway Korea/Tmoney app or KakaoMap/Naver Map (see Before You Go), it shows the current network plus real-time arrivals, more reliable than a static map here given how often lines get added.

PracticalStation lockers & luggage logistics

Checking out of a hotel or hanok in the morning with an evening train or flight still hours away? T-Locker lockers are built into 273 stations across the subway network, over 5,500 of them. Pay by credit card at the machine directly, no app or Korean phone number needed, though the T-Locker app makes booking a specific size ahead easier. Accessible any time the subway's running, roughly 5am-1am on weekdays, 5am-midnight at weekends.

For anything too big for a locker, or if you'd rather not carry it around at all, T-Luggage is a separate staffed service at larger stations: hand your bag to an attendant and either collect it later or have it delivered same-day to your next hotel. Pricier than a locker, but genuinely useful for day-tripping between cities without hauling a case around all day.

Mind the stairs: lifts exist but don't assume one's nearby or working. Big interchange stations, Express Bus Terminal and Dongdaemun History & Culture Park especially, mean long transfer walks and multiple flights of stairs between lines. Hauling a heavy suitcase through one of these at rush hour is a genuinely rough few minutes, worth timing transfers outside peak hours if you're travelling with a full case.

PracticalBikes, e-scooters & mopeds

Public bike share (Ttareungyi in Seoul, similar schemes in Busan and elsewhere) is the easy one: ₩1,000 for an hour, ₩5,000 for an unlimited-rides day pass, and since a 2026 update the app has full English onboarding, takes foreign cards directly, and doesn't need a Korean phone number. Genuinely tourist-ready.

Shared e-scooters (Swing, Kickgoing, Lime, Beam and similar) are more awkward than they look: most apps want a Korean phone number and registered ID, and from 2026 Seoul is tightening this further with mandatory driver's licence verification before they'll let you rent.

A helmet is legally required, riding on the pavement is banned, ride in a bike lane or at the road's right edge if there isn't one. Workable if you're already set up with a Korean number, not a reliable option to start from cold.

Mopeds and motorbikes are rentable, especially on Jeju near the airport, ferry terminals and the city centre, but reputable shops require, without exception, a passport, your home driving licence, and an International Driving Permit (1968 Vienna Convention, with a motorcycle/Category A endorsement) valid for up to a year on that combination.

Minimum age is typically 21, and 125cc-and-up bikes need a higher licence class. Expect a card security deposit against damage. Sort the IDP before you fly, it isn't issued locally.

PracticalTaxis: colours, deluxe & the airport option

Standard taxis (usually silver, white or orange) are colour-coded and highly affordable, book through Kakao T (see Before You Go) to skip the language barrier at the kerb entirely. Black "Model/Deluxe" taxis (Mobeom, 모범) cost noticeably more but come with more experienced drivers who often speak basic English, worth it for a late-night or long cross-city ride if the standard queue looks unreliable.

Seoul also runs a dedicated flat-rate Seoul International Taxi service (intltaxi.co.kr), bookable straight from the airport, with English-speaking drivers guaranteed. Useful if you land jet-lagged and would rather have the fare and the conversation sorted in advance than negotiated at the rank.

Reading the roof sign: it's the reverse of what you might expect. An illuminated red 빈차 (bin-cha, "empty car") sign means the taxi's free, hail it. If it instead reads 예약 (yeyak, "reserved"), someone's already booked it, don't rely on colour alone to tell the two apart, that sign can also show red, read the actual characters. If the sign's dark, it's already got a passenger.

Hidden GemRural buses: the "wild" village network (Nong-eo-chon)

Once you're off the subway and KTX network, in Yangyang, Guryongpo or anywhere similarly rural, you're in farming-and-fishing village bus territory (see Hidden Coastal Gems). Completely safe and legal, but a different set of rules: drivers move fast, routes shift without notice, and English signage is virtually non-existent.

The phone trick: destination signs on the bus front are Hangul-only. Type your destination into Papago (see Before You Go) in large font and show it to the driver as you board.

Wave to stop: rural stops are often just unmarked gravel patches on the roadside. The bus will speed past unless you physically stand at the road's edge and wave.

Carry cash: T-money is accepted in theory, but rural card readers frequently drop signal. Keep a stash of ₩1,000 notes as backup.

Early curfews: unlike Seoul's near-24-hour pulse, rural loop buses often stop by 19:00-20:00. Miss the last one and finding a rural taxi at night can take hours.

Three phrases worth having ready on your phone screen:

  • "Does this bus go to [Place]?" → 이 버스 [Place] 가나요?
  • "Please tell me when we arrive at [Place]." → [Place]에 도착하면 알려주세요.
  • "Please stop here." → 여기서 내려주세요.
